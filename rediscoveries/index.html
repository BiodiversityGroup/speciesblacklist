Life Overlooked

Thousands of species are slipping away without anyone counting them as at risk.

Conservation runs on lists. Money, legal protection and recovery plans all follow from a species having a name on the right one. This site is about the animals and plants that never made it onto any list, and what it will take to find them before the decision gets made for us.

Why anyone should care about a gap in a database

When a species is listed as threatened, things happen. Funders can point to it. Governments can be held to it. Researchers can build a case around it. When a species has no listing at all, none of that machinery can start, and the silence reads exactly like good news.

It very often is not good news. These are the species nobody has the information to worry about: known from one hillside, described once and never seen again, or named so recently that no assessor has reached them. If one of them goes extinct, it will happen without an entry, without a headline, and without anybody being able to say afterwards what was lost.

The Biodiversity Group built this site to work out who those species actually are. We call them Life Overlooked. Naming them is the first step, because you cannot fight for something that has never been counted.

What you will find here

Four separate pieces of work, each answering a different question. They come from different sources and they are deliberately never added together.

The core list

The Black List

Species the Red List has assessed and been unable to categorise, or has never assessed at all, ranked by how narrowly restricted the published record says they are. If a species here turns out to be in trouble, this is where you would have seen it first.

Described, then forgotten

New Species

Reptiles and amphibians named by science since 2015 that still carry no extinction risk assessment. Nine in ten are known from a single country. New ones join the queue every week.

Missing, not gone

Lost Species

Species nobody has confirmed alive in over a decade, and in some cases for centuries, that nobody has been able to declare extinct either. Half of them have not been seen since the 1960s.

Proof it can be done

Rediscoveries

Species found again, gathered as they are reported. This is the hopeful part. Most species that go missing are still out there, and some of them turn up because one person went and looked.

The part worth holding on to

A species being missing is not the same as a species being gone. Of the lost species that researchers have traced, roughly a quarter are thought to be genuinely extinct. That is a grim number read one way and a remarkable one read the other, because it means most of them are alive and simply unvisited.

Some have been unseen for two hundred years and then turned up in a stream nobody had surveyed. One was identified from a photograph a tourist posted online. The work is not hopeless. It is just unfunded, and pointed at the wrong places.

What would actually help

Three things, and none of them need you to be a scientist. Tell somebody about these species. Ask the people who decide where conservation money goes to include them. Support the groups already doing this work.

How to help

What this is

A species can be lost without ever having been counted as at risk.

The IUCN Red List is the world's authority on extinction risk, and it has two blind spots. Some species are assessed and come back Data Deficient, meaning IUCN looked and there was not enough information to say. Others are Not Evaluated, meaning nobody has looked at all. In practice both are treated as though the species were safe.

Why it matters that these species have no category

Neither category is covered by the commitment that counts. Target 4 of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework binds governments to halting the extinction of known threatened species. That wording excludes both of these groups by definition. The money follows the same line: under 3.5% of Mohamed bin Zayed Species Conservation Fund awards have gone to Data Deficient taxa, and under 1% at the People's Trust for Endangered Species.

IUCN's own guidance says Data Deficient species should receive the same attention as threatened ones. It is not followed. So the gap is not only a gap in knowledge. It is an exclusion written into policy and funding, and it is the reason for compiling this register at all.

What the tiers mean

Every species is placed in one of five restriction tiers according to how narrow its published record is. That is the only thing the tier measures, and it is the axis the whole register is ranked on. A smaller known range is what would make a species qualify as threatened under IUCN Criterion B. Tiers 3, 4 and 5 are the priority stratum, the group whose ranking has been tested against IUCN's own reassessments. The tested break in risk falls between tier 2 and tier 3, not higher up.

    Read the ranking before you use it

    Data Deficient status on its own predicts very little. Of the species here that IUCN has since reassessed, the group as a whole came out threatened at roughly the ordinary all-species rate. The narrowly restricted subset is different, and that distinction, not the Data Deficient label, is what this register is for.

    This is a ranked subset, not a census. IUCN v2026‑1 holds 8,659 Data Deficient species in vertebrate classes; this register covers 3,031 of them, which is 35%. The method needs a published sentence describing each species' range, and the corpus behind it is one reference work. Of the 2,964 Data Deficient vertebrates that corpus names, every one is in the register; the other 5,628 are never named in it at all. So the right reading is "of the Data Deficient vertebrates with a usable published range statement, these are the most narrowly restricted", not "these are the ones that matter most".

    How long is a blank allowed to stand? The Red List export says only that a species is Data Deficient now. Its assessment history, pulled from IUCN's API, says since when, and the answer reframes the register. The median species here has been Data Deficient for 16 years; 475 have held the label for twenty years or more. And 1,210 of them, 40%, have been assessed more than once, meaning IUCN returned, looked again, and wrote Data Deficient a second or third time. Those are not species awaiting a first look. For scale, the median time IUCN has actually taken to resolve a Data Deficient listing is 16 years, so 1,011 species in this register are already past it.

    The species outside were screened out, not overlooked. Richardson takes an explicit position on the Data Deficient category: most of it, in his judgement, is junior synonyms or valid species not in immediate danger, while genuinely threatened species get lost there for want of follow-up research. He admits a Data Deficient species when its record is narrow enough to imply risk, and that screen is measurable, since he kept 28% of IUCN's Data Deficient ray-finned fishes against 72% of the birds, dropping the deep-sea groups almost entirely because naturally rare is not the same as threatened. So the omitted species are neither unexamined nor shown to be safe: one author's judgement, made without the research he says the category lacks, is not an assessment. It also means this corpus is pre-selected for the trait the register ranks on, which is why 92% of records state some restriction. The register tests an expert's screen against IUCN's later verdicts rather than discovering the pattern from scratch.

    Held-out test

    Does the ranking agree with IUCN's own verdict?

    Between the previous data and v2026‑1, IUCN independently reassessed 108 of these species and gave each a real category. The ranking rules never saw those outcomes, so they work as an answer key. Two caveats belong with the result: it rests on 28 species in the priority stratum, so the interval matters more than the point estimate; and both the ranking and the assessment draw on the same primary literature, so this is agreement between two readings of one evidence base rather than a blind prediction.     Does that depend on trusting IUCN's “not threatened” calls? It is a fair worry: the Red List Authorities have been working the Data Deficient backlog down through specialist-group consensus rather than new fieldwork, so a species can be moved to Least Concern because nobody reported a threat. Tested by discarding the thinnest-evidenced calls, the result gets stronger, not weaker. Two measures agree. By specimen record, 53.6% becomes 62.5% when calls resting on five or fewer records worldwide are set aside. By citation, pulling every assessment's own reference list from IUCN's API, not one of the 140 reassessments cites nothing newer than the Data Deficient listing it replaced; and restricting the test to the ones citing new research about that particular species gives 83.3%. What the citation check also shows, and this is the real concession: most of these reassessments cite no new species-specific research at all, 74 of 101 non-threatened outcomes. That is how sparse-data assessment works, and it applies about equally to the threatened outcomes (26 of 39), so it does not tilt the comparison.     This maps collecting effort as much as biology

    A dense patch means records exist there, which is not the same as species being concentrated there. Records pile up around universities, museums, expedition routes and coastlines somebody surveyed. Read the map as a picture of where the evidence sits, not of where the risk is.

    This is not a backlog. It is a rate.

     

    A backlog is a pile you can work through. This is a tap that has been left on. Clearing every species on both lists tomorrow would leave the same number arriving the year after, so the question is not when will somebody catch up, but what would it take to keep up at all.

    The reptile list carries two columns this one cannot: a restriction tier and the finer locality detail behind it. Those are derived from the Reptile Database's per-species distribution text, and AmphibiaWeb's listing gives a country rather than a locality, so the columns are absent rather than guessed. In their place is the describing publication, which the reptile list has only as an authority string.

    The list

    How this list was built, and what it cannot tell you

    Where the species come from

    The Reptile Database checklist of June 2026 lists 12,650 accepted species. Comparing it with IUCN v2026-1, which holds 10,305 assessed reptiles, leaves the species the Red List has no record of.

    Comparing names alone would be unreliable. A species often gets moved into a different genus, so its first name changes while the animal stays the same, and the two organisations do not always agree on where it belongs. So species are matched either on the full scientific name or on who first described it and in what year. Those are details that stay with a species through every change of name. That caught 169 species a name-only comparison would have wrongly reported as missing.

    Why you can trust the “never assessed” claim

    Failing to find a name in a search does not prove nobody ever assessed the species, because it might be filed under a different name. So every species here was looked up individually in the Red List's own database, and every name it did not return was checked again against GBIF's list of alternative names for the same species. That found 51 which had been assessed after all, and they were removed: an error rate of 2.9% in the list before checking.

    What “extent” means, and the ranking that failed

    Extent is taken from each species' own published distribution: the country, state or landform it is recorded from. That is the measure the evidence supports : species endemic to a single country turn out to be threatened roughly twice as often as those crossing borders (27.2% against 13.2%, p = 0.00005, tested on 600 reptiles the Red List has assessed).

    A finer, coordinate-based scale was tried first and abandoned. Its top class, living only on an island, turned out to predict nothing at all : island species were no more likely to be threatened than any other (odds ratio 0.92, p = 1: as close to no effect as a result gets). The reason is that “island” taken from a written description covers a one-square-kilometre rock and Hainan's 34,000 km² alike. Geocoding the locality text could not rescue it either: measured against species whose descriptions print coordinates, it was off by a median of 53 km and in the worst case 5,469 km.

    The “finer detail” column counts how many records carry map coordinates, where GBIF has any. It is descriptive only and has not been validated against Red List outcomes. For a species described last year, few records may mean a small range, or may only mean nobody has looked twice.

    Why there are no coordinates here

    This page gives each species' distribution and no more. Exact type localities and coordinates are deliberately absent, including from the downloadable data.

    These are narrowly restricted reptiles the Red List has never listed, so no legal protection follows from a listing, and island endemics among them are exactly what the reptile trade targets. Scattered across separate descriptions their localities are hard to exploit; gathered into one file they become a collecting list. Describers know this, and obscuring a type locality in a new reptile description is common practice for the same reason. Researchers who need locality data should go to the original descriptions, where the authors decided for themselves what to disclose.

    How this differs from the register

    The register asks which assessed species IUCN looked at and returned we don't know about, and its ranking is tested against IUCN's own later reassessments. This page asks who is missing from the Red List entirely. It draws on a different source and uses a different instrument, so the two lists must never be added together.

    Species nobody has seen for a very long time

    2,135 species that have not been recorded in years, in some cases in centuries, and that nobody has been able to declare extinct. They are not gone. They are unaccounted for, which is a different thing, and the difference is where the work is.

    What "lost" means here

    Missing
    Unseen in the wild for more than 5 but fewer than 10 years, with no population held in captivity or cultivation.
    Lost
    Not confirmed alive by photographic, audio or genetic evidence for more than 10 years. This is the threshold for everything on this page: 816 species qualify.
    Long lost
    Approximately 50 years unobserved. Most of this list, 1,135 species, is in this category, and a further 182 carry too little date information to be placed in either.

    Definitions from Long & Rodríguez 2022, Oryx 56(4), "Lost but not forgotten", which is the vocabulary the underlying list uses. Note what they turn on: evidence, not conservation status. A species can be missing for a decade and still be assessed as fine: 208 species here are currently listed Least Concern, and another 462 are Data Deficient.

    Where they were last seen

    Each circle is a country or region, sized by how many lost species have their range recorded there. Hover or tap for the breakdown.

    What this map is not

    A circle sits at a country's centre point, not at a locality. It says "this many lost species have their range given as this country". it is not a sighting, not a range map, and not a search area. Large countries accumulate large circles partly because they are large.

    The list is also uneven by design. It inherits the Re:wild / IUCN SSC lost species list, which is far better resolved for vertebrates than for insects, plants or molluscs. Reptiles, fish and amphibians make up more than half of it. Reading the map as a picture of the world's lost biodiversity would mistake the shape of the list for the shape of the problem.

    Where we lose them is not where we find them

    This site carries two maps: species that have gone missing, and species being described for the first time. Set side by side they disagree, and the disagreement is the argument. Brazil has lost the most and is still describing plenty. China is describing more new species than anywhere on earth and carries almost no backlog. The United States is the reverse: a long list of losses and very few new descriptions.

    The longest wait in each group

     

    The groups are nothing like each other, though: a bird goes missing for a decade and it is news, while a snail can go missing for a century before anyone notices it has. These are the record holders, the species in each group whose last confirmed record is the oldest of all.

    What had to be thrown out to build this table

    Taking simply the oldest date in each group produced a table we could not stand behind. Ten of the first twenty-one entries were contradicted by GBIF, the global occurrence database, showing the animal turning up long after the date this list gives as its last.

    The fish was the worst of them. Astyanax scabripinnis, a Brazilian tetra, sits in the source list with a last record of 1842. GBIF holds 1,488 occurrences of it, the most recent from 2018. It is not a lost species. The same was true of Cycloramphus fuliginosus, a frog with 152 records running to 2014, and of eight others.

    Each group therefore shows its oldest candidate that GBIF does not contradict, and all 23 rejections are recorded with their reasons. Two cautions had to be built into that check. Evidence counts only where the scientific name matches exactly. An apparent 7,537 records for one spider turned out to be a loose match to a common European species. And an absence of recent records proves nothing either: a Sierra Leone crab had none, and had still been found again. These are errors in the lost-species list, not in the Red List, and they are why this page treats its own inputs as claims to be checked rather than facts to be republished.

    Counting is not the same as rate

     

    What that rate is, and is not

    It is not a rate of loss. The numerator is a curated list rather than a census, and the denominator is how many species each group's own taxonomic authority currently recognises. Effort is the confound, and it runs one way: birds are the best-surveyed group on earth, so a low bird rate partly measures how hard people have looked rather than how well birds are doing.

    Only vertebrate groups appear, covering … of the species on this page. The rest are invertebrates and plants, where the lost list is far less complete and a rate would be comparing our ignorance with theirs. Frogs and toads are shown together here because AmphibiaWeb publishes Anura as a single figure, and a guessed split is worse than an honest merge.

    Two words doing more work than they should

    "Toad" is not a group. Everyday usage covers warty-skinned frogs in at least six families, so the split here is the strict one: toads means the true toads, family Bufonidae, and every other frog is filed as a frog. A spadefoot toad will therefore appear under frogs, which is correct taxonomically and wrong conversationally. "Fish" is the same kind of convenience, covering three classes, kept together because that is the question people ask. Groups come from GBIF's backbone taxonomy, matched on the scientific name.

    Why some species come back and others never do

    Rediscovery is not random, and it is not simply a matter of looking harder. Lindken and colleagues examined every lost tetrapod they could trace and found that the species which come back share a profile: they tend to be large, conspicuous mammals and birds, tolerant of a range of habitats, living near people, in countries with money for fieldwork.

    The species that stay lost are the mirror image. Narrow range. Small, cryptic, nocturnal, or underground. And, the finding that matters most here, living somewhere that is hard or dangerous to reach, so nobody has searched. A species can remain "lost" for a century without any evidence that it has declined at all. It can simply be that no qualified person has been to the right ridge in the right month.

    Their overall estimate is that roughly a quarter of lost species are probably already extinct. Which is a bleak number read one way, and an extraordinary one read the other: it implies that most of the species on this map are still out there.

    They also found that tetrapods are being lost faster than they are being found again. Rediscovery is slowing for amphibians, birds and mammals, while reptiles are being lost quickly. The gap is widening, and it will not be closed by the kind of survey that finds large animals near roads.

    Plenty do come back. Of the tetrapods Lindken and colleagues traced, 424 of 1,280, a third of them, had already been found again by the time they published. This page carries the ones still missing, so a rediscovery is a species leaving it. The longest documented wait on record here is a Sierra Leone freshwater crab, Afrithelphusa afzelii, refound after 225 years. Recent finds are collected on the Rediscoveries page.

    What The Biodiversity Group does about it

    The Biodiversity Group works in the places the profile above predicts will be overlooked: steep, wet, unglamorous forest, a long way from a field station, looking for small animals at night. Three of those records are on this map.

    Ecuador · 2019

    Fugler's Shadow Snake

    Emmochliophis fugleri

    Known from a single specimen collected in 1966 and never seen again. On 7 March 2019, at the Río Manduriacu Reserve in Imbabura, The Biodiversity Group's Scott Trageser and Ross Maynard found one: the second individual of this species ever recorded, and the first photographed alive. The rediscovery was published in 2021, 54 years after the first.

    The paper recommends the species be listed Critically Endangered, because the reserve where it was found is under imminent threat from mining. The IUCN still lists it as Data Deficient, assessed in 2016 and not revisited since it was found again.

    Maynard et al. 2021, Check List 17(1)

    Vietnam · 2014

    A land snail found by a stranger online

    Myxostoma petiverianum

    The last published record was Dautzenberg and Fischer's, in 1905. On 3 August 2014, on a citizen-science survey called Con Dao Land and Sea, The Biodiversity Group's Scott Trageser photographed a snail on Hòn Cau that nobody present could name, and posted it to iNaturalist. That was the whole method.

    In November 2015 a malacologist in Malaysia, Junn Kitt Foon, came across the photograph there and recognised the animal, the first ever taken of a live adult. No expedition and no budget found this species. A picture left somewhere public did, and one specialist who happened to scroll past it.

    Trageser, Wiscovitch & Foon 2018, The Festivus 50(2): 110–113 · Mongabay, which reports the 1905 date

    Ecuador · 2018

    The Mindo Glassfrog

    Nymphargus balionota

    Seen once since 1984, a single male in 2005. In 2018 a Biodiversity Group team was at Río Manduriacu surveying the Tandayapa Andes toad when Scott Trageser, too ill to join the night survey, went to a stream near camp instead and heard a frog he did not recognise calling for a mate.

    Return surveys in 2019 found more in three separate streams, including the first females ever recorded, plus metamorphs and egg masses. Not a survivor, then: a breeding population.

    Reported in Amphibian & Reptile Conservation, July 2020

    The list is not a finished thing

    Two open questions sit on it, and both need a taxonomist rather than a spreadsheet. Fifteen names are treated by one authority as valid species and by another as synonyms of something else. When a narrow endemic is absorbed into a widespread species, it leaves conservation attention by clerical means rather than by evidence. A further 149 were removed from the source list by IUCN SSC specialists for reasons that were never recorded per species: some because they had been found, some because nobody had ever looked. Those two reasons point in opposite directions, and the difference is the whole argument.

    Species from this list that have turned up again in the news are collected on the Rediscoveries page.

    Another list, counting something else

    Recently Extinct Plants and Animals is the most thorough catalogue of its kind we know of, with 12,859 taxa, painstakingly sourced and with a citation behind almost every entry. It is worth your time, and it is worth saying exactly how it differs from this page, because the two look like the same subject and are not.

    Its rediscoveries section counts species that disappeared from a country and were later refound there, such as a plant gone from the British flora for ninety years, turning up on one roadside. This page counts species missing from science, everywhere, at once. Both are real losses and both matter; they are simply different questions.

    The gap is measurable. We compared the two: of 1,063 species in their rediscoveries listing, exactly one also appears among the 2,135 here: Amphilophus lyonsi, a Costa Rican cichlid, and it has indeed been found again. That near-total absence is not a disagreement between the lists. It is the two definitions doing their jobs.

    Found again

    Researchers and naturalists keep finding species that were feared lost. Here are … of those finds.

    A species is called lost when it has gone unrecorded for so long that scientists no longer know whether it survives. Some of them are still out there, and researchers, park rangers and amateur naturalists keep proving it, by going back into the forest, the cave or the reef and finding them.

    This page gathers those finds as they are reported, checked every day. It records what was reported, not what has been confirmed. A news story is a lead, not proof. There is a note below the list explaining what that means.

    The finds

    Newest first. Search looks at the species name, the headline, the summary and the outlet, so try a species, a country or a news outlet.

    Where these come from, and what they do and don’t prove

    What is on this page

    Journal alerts, conservation outlets and news searches run across fourteen national editions are read once a day. Anything describing a species being found again is kept, and checked against the thousands of species names this project follows, so where a report names a species we can say whether it was one we were already watching.

    The national editions matter more than they sound. A news search returns a different set of results in each country, and running only the United States edition missed 60 of 134 stories on a single query, among them Wallace's giant bee, the golden mole refound in South Africa and Attenborough's echidna. Rediscovery happens mostly outside the United States, so a US-only search is close to the worst way to look for it. The searches are still English-language only, which remains a real gap.

    One find is usually covered by many outlets, each writing its own headline, so the same story arrives over and over. Stories are matched up by the words they share and shown once, with a count of how many outlets ran it. That count says how much attention the find got, not how solid it is.

    How far back this goes, and why the recent years look busier

    The oldest report here is from August 2020 and the newest from July 2026. The recent years hold far more of them, with roughly three quarters falling in 2025 and 2026, and that is a property of the search, not of the world. A news index surfaces recent material and only a thinning sample of older material, so nothing on this page should be read as rediscoveries becoming more frequent.

    The rate at which species are actually found again is a different question, and one this page cannot answer. The archive only grows forwards from here, so coverage will be even across future years and permanently sparse before 2025.

    How we work out which species a story is about

    Species have two-word scientific names, like Chelonoidis phantasticus for the Fernandina tortoise. Every scientific name shown here has been looked up in a global database of species names to confirm the animal or plant really exists. It is not enough for a phrase to simply look like one.

    Headlines often name nothing at all. “Five lost bird species rediscovered” tells you there are five, but not which five, so the article itself is read to find them.

    That makes some names surer than others, and they are shown differently. A name in this colour came from the headline or the summary, or was used by two separate outlets. A name in grey, after “also names”, was found further down in one article, and articles often mention a close relative for comparison, which can look just like the species that was found. Treat the grey ones as leads worth checking.

    Only the names are kept; the article's text is read and then discarded.

    What happens when these articles disappear

    News sites reorganise, paywall and delete, and image paths churn faster than the articles do. Every card here depends on a page nobody at this project controls, so what can be preserved is preserved, and it is worth being exact about how much that is, because the three parts are not equally safe.

    • The words: all of them. Headline, summary, outlet and date are stored here and are what each card is built from. No card needs its article to be alive in order to describe it.
    • The picture: most of them. Preview images are copied to this site and served from it, so they outlive the publisher's own copy. Where that copy failed the card still loads the original, and those are the ones that will go blank one day.
    • A link that still works: seldom. Where the Internet Archive already holds a snapshot, the card offers it alongside the original. Most of these articles have never been archived by anyone, and this page does not submit them.

    The article text itself is read, which is how the species in a roundup get identified, but it is discarded immediately and never stored. Keeping a copy would be republishing someone else's work. The job of this page is to send readers to the journalism, not to stand in for it.

    Why some cards are text only

    Most cards show the publisher’s own headline image and summary. Some show only text, and the reason is worth stating plainly: a growing number of news sites forbid automated readers in the file where a site declares such things. Reuters and The Sun bar every automated visitor; the Washington Post, the Telegraph, MSN and even BirdLife International name AI readers specifically. This page respects that and does not fetch those pages.

    Where the Internet Archive holds a copy, the summary comes from there instead and the card links to it. Where it does not, the card carries the headline, the outlet and the date, which is enough to go and read it yourself, and is the point of the link.

    What a report here does not establish

    These are news items. A rediscovery is only settled by a specimen, a photograph or a sequence that specialists accept, published somewhere durable, and some reports on this page will not survive that test. Others describe a population found by people who never considered it lost in the first place.

    Nothing here feeds the register or the counts elsewhere on this site. Those figures come from the IUCN Red List and from checked sources, and a press report has never been allowed to change one.

    Corrections

    If a species on this page was never lost, if a rediscovery has since been retracted, or if one is missing, please say so: business@biodiversitygroup.org.

    How to help

    These species are not short of scientific interest. They are short of attention. Three things move that, and only one of them requires any expertise.

    1. Tell somebody

    The single most useful thing anyone can do is make these species less obscure. Conservation funding follows attention, and attention follows people talking. A species that nobody has heard of does not get a recovery plan.

    Share this site. Send a page to somebody who would find it interesting. If you work anywhere near this field, put it in front of the person who signs off your grants.

    Something you can post as it is:

    There is a list of animals and plants that conservation has effectively lost track of. Not endangered. Not extinct. Just unaccounted for.

    Some have not been seen since the 1800s. Nobody can declare them gone, so nobody can protect them either. Most of them are probably still out there.

    Someone has finally started writing them all down: speciesblacklist.org

    2. Ask for these species to be prioritised

    Every species on this site is excluded from the commitments that drive conservation spending, because those commitments are written around species already known to be threatened. Changing that is a matter of people asking for it, repeatedly, in the places where lists get written.

    • If you fund conservation: ask whether your criteria quietly rule out species with no assessment. Most do, without anyone intending it.
    • If you work for an agency or a government: Data Deficient and unassessed species can be written into national priorities even though the global targets skip them.
    • If you are a researcher: an assessment is a publishable contribution, and these are the species where one assessment changes the whole picture for a taxon.
    • If you are a conservationist: a survey that produces no sighting is still a result worth reporting, and almost nobody publishes those.

    3. Support the people already doing it

    This work is unglamorous and it is nearly always unfunded. Finding a small brown frog on a steep hillside at two in the morning does not photograph like a tiger, and the organisations that do it are small.

    The Biodiversity Group built this site and works on exactly these species. If you would like to back that, or do it with us, both doors are open:

    • Donate, which pays for the fieldwork that turns a name on this list into a record.
    • Get involved, including expeditions and internships if you want to be there when it happens.

    If your money or your time is better placed with another group working on neglected species, place it there instead. The point is the species, not us.

    And if you have seen one of them

    That is the whole reason this list is public. If you have a record, a photograph or a museum specimen of anything on these pages, or you know a species has been found again, please tell us: business@biodiversitygroup.org. One of the rediscoveries on this site began as a photograph posted online by somebody who had no idea what they had found.

    Methods & limits

    Who called these species "priority"?

    Nobody with a specialist's authority. The ranking is produced by a text-classification rule that reads the source's own range statements and sorts them by severity. No taxonomist or conservation biologist has reviewed the individual species. What has been tested is the rule's average behaviour against IUCN's independent reassessments — a statement about the group, not about any one row.

    These are not Red List assessments and must not be cited as such. They are a prioritised research agenda: an argument about where to look.

    Why the gap matters

    Target 4 of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework commits governments to halting the extinction of known threatened species — a phrase that excludes both categories in this register by definition. Funding follows the same line: under 3.5% of Mohamed bin Zayed Species Conservation Fund awards have gone to Data Deficient taxa, and under 1% at the People's Trust for Endangered Species. IUCN's own guidance says Data Deficient species should get the same attention as threatened ones, and it is not followed.

    So the consequence of having no category is not merely that a species is poorly known. It is excluded from the target that mobilises national action and from the funds that pay for fieldwork — which is what makes deciding which of these species to look at first worth doing carefully.

    What a restriction tier is

    Every species is assigned one of five restriction tiers. The tier records one thing only: how narrow the species' published record is, read from the range statement in Richardson's own text. Tier 5 means the species is known from a single specimen or nothing beyond its type locality; tier 1 means the source makes no restriction claim at all.

    What a tier is not: it is not an IUCN category, not a threat level, not a measure of how endangered the species is, and not a count of individuals. Two species in the same tier may face completely different pressures. The tier is a statement about the evidence, not about the animal.

    Restriction is the axis because IUCN Criterion B allows a species to qualify as threatened on a small range plus evidence of decline: area of occupancy under 2,000 km² for Vulnerable, 500 for Endangered, 10 for Critically Endangered. A species known only from its type locality sits, on its face, inside the Critically Endangered envelope. That is why a narrow record is worth ranking on. Tiers 3, 4 and 5 together are the priority stratum, and they are the only part of the ranking that has been tested. The boundary sits there because that is where the tested risk actually jumps — tier 2 came out threatened at 20%, tier 3 at 67% — and not because the top of the scale is the most severe: it is not, see Methods.

    What the test showed

    The priority rate lands close to the 56% that Borgelt et al. (2022, Communications Biology) predicted for Data Deficient species generally — which suggests that figure belongs to the narrowly restricted subset, and that an unranked list dilutes a real signal with vague-range species.

    Two caveats belong with that number whenever it is quoted. The top stratum rests on 19 species, which is thin. And IUCN reassesses where data has appeared, which probably favours species that turned out to be more widespread — a bias running against the finding rather than creating it. Separately, the five tiers were defined before any outcome was seen, but the decision to split them at 4 was made after, which inflates the significance somewhat.

    Where localities come from

    A locality is recorded two ways, and the Localities page distinguishes them.

    From a locality account. Richardson organises the book geographically, so most species sit inside a paragraph introduced by a place. That place, and its opening sentence, are attached to every species in the paragraph. This is the stronger provenance and it covers 519 species.

    From the species entry itself. Where a species is not inside a locality account, the place is very often named in its own sentence anyway — "collected from the upper Pungwe River", "collected off Mauritius". Pattern extraction over those sentences recovers a locality for a further 1,213 species, taking coverage from 19% to 60% of the register and the site list from 42 to 102. Clusters that were entirely invisible before appear this way — the Gulf of California holds 19 species, none of which had a locality until now.

    Named physical features (Salween River, Gulf of Panama, Mount Kinabalu) are preferred over a bare capitalised word after a preposition, and a capture is rejected if it collides with the species' own common name or genus. The place is only ever taken from the text, never guessed — if the sentence names no place, the species keeps no locality, which is why 1,330 still have none.

    These names are then looked up in a gazetteer to place them on the map, which is a separate step with its own failure modes and its own verification — see Geocoding the locality names below. Nothing in the register or on the Localities page depends on that lookup: those show the name the source gave, and the coordinates exist only for the map.

    How the map is built, and what it is not

    The register stores localities as text taken from Richardson's prose — "The Mano River", "Arrowsmith Bank", "Espiritu Santo". Those were not run through a gazetteer to make the map: place-name geocoding is ambiguous exactly where this corpus is ambiguous, and a map that confidently places a species in the wrong ocean is worse than no map. The points are real museum specimens and field sightings that already carry map coordinates — “georeferenced” records — taken from GBIF and queried per species.

    One trap governed that query, and it is the same species of error that wrecked the first never-assessed list. GBIF's free-text name search must not be used, because many of these names are synonyms and it silently returns records of the accepted species instead:

    Anampses viridis, free-text search 3,071 recordscentroid inland in New South Wales
    Anampses viridis, strict taxon key 3 recordsall at Réunion

    The book describes that species as known from a single specimen; the 3,071 belong to Anampses caeruleopunctatus, its widespread senior synonym. Every name is therefore resolved to a GBIF usage key by strict match first, and records are pulled by taxon key. Records GBIF itself flags as having bad coordinates are excluded, and points are capped at 30 per species so well-collected species cannot dominate the surface.

    What the map is not. Not range maps — these are point records. Not a picture of where risk is concentrated — it maps collecting effort at least as much as biology, because georeferenced records cluster where institutions and expeditions have worked. And not the same basis as the Localities page, which comes from the book rather than from GBIF, so the two will not correspond exactly. Some bad coordinates survive the filtering, which is why the page shows density rather than authoritative dots.

    Geocoding the locality names

    The register's place names are also plotted, as rings, because refusing to look up "Salween River" while happily printing it was inconsistent. But a gazetteer answers any string, and two checks are needed, not one.

    Type agreement catches the obvious failures: asked for "The Cordillera Central", Nominatim returns a university in Baguio. A name ending "River" must come back as a waterway, "Island" as an island; buildings, roads and campuses are rejected outright. That removed — of — candidates.

    But type agreement cannot see a same-type homonym, and those are common. "Congo River" resolved to a real river of that name in Sierra Leone, 4,000 km from the Congo, passing cleanly as river/river. "Dunk Island" resolved near Sydney rather than Queensland. So each geocode is then measured against the GBIF records of the species that sit at that locality — an independent witness already held:

    Agree within 500 km — plotted —
    Disagree by more — discarded —
    No species with records to check against — off by default —

    Around one in five checkable geocodes was wrong by more than 500 km, and none of those errors was detectable from the name or the feature type alone. That rate is the reason the unverified — are not plotted by default: there is no reason to think they are cleaner, and they cannot be tested. The validated rings add — species that have no georeferenced record of their own — modest, but every one is corroborated.

    A locality naming two places is kept but never plotted. — strings in the register are compounds — "Athi and Tana River", "Caroline and Marshall Islands". There is no single coordinate for them, and every way of reducing one to a single place either drops the noun that identified it ("Caroline") or manufactures somewhere that does not exist ("Oman and Masirah Island" → "Oman Island"). They are left whole, excluded from the gazetteer, and the evidence sentence shows both places.

    The most telling number is the one that cannot be plotted. Of the 3,062 species here, 805 have no georeferenced record anywhere in GBIF, and 727 cannot be placed even by a cross-checked locality. They are the least visible members of an already invisible group, and they are absent from the map.

    An earlier version of this page said 943 rather than 629. That figure was wrong: the occurrence fetch had been running eight concurrent requests, GBIF answered 399 of them with HTTP 429, and the script cached those failures as though they were results — so re-running never retried them and 16.6% of the register was silently recorded as having no records. Because the register is sorted by tier, the failures landed disproportionately on tier 1, which appeared to be 3.6% mappable when it is in fact 80%. The fetcher now backs off exponentially, uses four threads, and never caches a failure.

    What was discarded

    An earlier composite score added points for a species not having been recorded recently and for threats named at its locality. Testing destroyed it: the composite was non-monotonic, and naming a site-level threat turned out inversely associated with being threatened. The reason is legible in hindsight — broad, well-documented regions attract threat prose but hold wide-ranging species, while a genuine type-locality endemic gets one terse line. Those fields survive on each record as description, and are not ranked on.

    Why the never-assessed list is short

    Proving that IUCN has never assessed a species is much harder than it looks, because the two sources disagree about naming constantly. Deciding it by failing to find a name produced a false-positive rate near 90%. Four distinct failure modes had to be closed:

    FailureExample
    Genus reassignedAmblyopsis rosae is IUCN's Troglichthys rosae (Near Threatened)
    Genus and family reassignedAphanius iberus is IUCN's Apricaphanius iberus
    Homonym within an orderSinosuthora przewalskii, an Asian parrotbill, matching Grallaria przewalskii, a South American antpitta
    Rank changeCyanoramphus cookii, in fact Least Concern

    Species known only from pre-1500 remains were also removed. The Red List covers extinctions from 1500 AD onward, so those fall outside its remit by policy rather than by neglect — counting them as coverage gaps would be a category error.

    Limits worth stating plainly

    • Vertebrates only. Invertebrates, plants and fungi are where the assessment gap is worst — invertebrates are 97% of animals and about a third of animal assessments — and this register says nothing about them.
    • One source. Everything rests on a single author's synthesis. No second corpus corroborates the range statements.
    • Restriction is read from prose, not from range data.
    • Localities vary in grain, from one river to an ocean basin.
